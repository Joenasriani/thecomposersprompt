From notation to specification, layered compositional prompts, and the place of theoretical knowledge in generative composition.

Chapter 1: The Composer's New Instrument

1.1 From Notation to Specification

A musical score is a specification: it states the pitch, duration, and articulation of every note, leaving the performer to realize the sound. The score for a Beethoven symphony does not contain the symphony; it contains the instructions from which a competent orchestra can produce the symphony. The composer's genius lies in the precision of the specification, and the performer's artistry lies in the interpretation of what the specification leaves open—tempo rubato, dynamic shading, timbral color.

An AI music prompt is also a specification, but of a different kind. It does not state individual notes; it states constraints and intentions—key, tempo, genre, mood, instrumentation, harmonic vocabulary—and the model generates audio that satisfies those constraints to a greater or lesser degree. A prompt that says "melancholic indie-folk in A minor, 90 BPM, fingerpicked guitar" is not a score; it is a request for the model to draw from its training data a track that matches those parameters. The model's output is not a performance of the prompt; it is a sample from the probability distribution the prompt defines.

The difference matters because the model's "reading" of the specification is probabilistic, not deterministic. A score that says "C4, quarter note" will be played as C4, quarter note, by any competent performer. A prompt that says "melancholic indie-folk in A minor" may generate a track in A minor, or it may drift toward D Dorian because "melancholic" and "indie-folk" carry their own harmonic associations in the training data. It may follow the chord progression you specify, or it may substitute a progression that co-occurs more frequently with the genre label. The composer's task is to constrain the probability space as tightly as the theory allows, and then to read the output as evidence of where the constraints held and where they failed.

This is not a limitation of the technology; it is the nature of the instrument. A violin has a range, a timbre, and a set of techniques; a composer who writes outside that range produces nothing. An AI music model has a training distribution, a set of learned associations, and a prompt interface; a composer who writes outside that distribution produces generic output. The theory is what allows the composer to stay within the instrument's range while still producing something specific.

1.2 The Prompt as a Layered Score

The most effective prompts are layered: they move from the general to the specific, and they restate critical constraints at multiple points. This is not redundancy for its own sake; it is a response to the probabilistic nature of the model. A single mention of the key may be honored or ignored; a key declaration in the Style box, reinforced by bracketed chords in the lyrics, creates two independent signals that the model must reconcile. The more layers, the tighter the constraint.

Consider this layered prompt for Suno:

Style: Gritty analog synth bass, with a driving 8th-note arpeggio, in A minor, 8 bars, 128 BPM. Lyrics: [Am] … [F] … [C] … [G]

The Style box carries the timbre ("gritty analog synth bass"), the behavior ("driving 8th-note arpeggio"), the key ("A minor"), the structure ("8 bars"), and the tempo ("128 BPM"). The lyrics carry the harmonic progression as bracketed chord symbols. The two layers reinforce each other: the key declaration in the Style box and the bracketed chords in the lyrics both specify the harmonic container, and the redundancy is deliberate. If the model drifts toward a different key, the bracketed chords pull it back; if the model ignores the chords, the key declaration keeps the output diatonic.

The same layering principle applies across genres. A lo-fi hip hop prompt might read:

Style: Lo-fi hip hop, 85 BPM, D minor key, swung rhythm, vinyl crackle, jazzy piano chords, mellow bass, tape hiss, soft hi-hats, rain ambience, instrumental only Lyrics: [Dm9] … [Gm7] … [B♭maj7] … [A7sus4]

Here the Style box specifies the genre ("lo-fi hip hop"), the tempo ("85 BPM"), the key ("D minor"), the feel ("swung rhythm"), the production texture ("vinyl crackle," "tape hiss"), the instrumentation ("jazzy piano chords," "mellow bass," "soft hi-hats"), the ambience ("rain ambience"), and the vocal presence ("instrumental only"). The lyrics specify the harmonic progression with extended chords that are idiomatic to the genre. A cinematic orchestral prompt might read:

Style: Cinematic orchestral, 72 BPM, C minor key, sweeping strings, French horn melody, timpani rolls, building from solo piano to full orchestra Lyrics: [Cm] … [A♭] … [E♭] … [B♭] … [Cm]

And a synthwave prompt:

Style: Synthwave, 110 BPM, F# minor key, analog synth arpeggios, gated reverb drums, warm bass, retro 80s production Lyrics: [F#m] … [D] … [A] … [E]

In each case, the prompt is not a single instruction but a stack of constraints, each of which narrows the probability space. The composer's skill lies in choosing which constraints to specify, at what level of precision, and in what order—and then in reading the output to see which constraints survived.

1.3 Why Theory Matters More, Not Less

One might expect that AI music generation would make music theory less important. After all, if the model can generate a track from a vague prompt, why bother learning about Roman numerals, modal interchange, or voice leading? The opposite is true. When the composer wrote every note, the theory was implicit in the notation. When the composer writes a prompt, the theory must be explicit in the specification. The model does not infer the theory from the notation; it infers it from the words. The composer must therefore know the theory well enough to name it.

Consider the difference between a vague prompt and a theoretical one:

Vague prompt:

Style: "Sad song"

Theoretical prompt:

Style: "Lonely Brooklyn rooftop, 2009, 90 BPM, raspy male, half-singing, A minor key, Am–F–C–G progression, fingerpicked acoustic guitar, ambient city noise"

The first averages across ten thousand labeled sad songs. It will produce something that sounds sad in a generic way—perhaps a slow piano ballad, perhaps a minor-key acoustic track, perhaps something with strings. The second gives the model "way less guessing to do." It specifies the scene ("lonely Brooklyn rooftop, 2009"), the tempo ("90 BPM"), the vocal texture ("raspy male, half-singing"), the key ("A minor"), the progression ("Am–F–C–G"), the instrumentation ("fingerpicked acoustic guitar"), and the ambience ("ambient city noise"). The theory is what allows the composer to replace vague adjectives with concrete parameters.

The same principle applies across genres. A vague electronic prompt ("Electronic dance music") might produce anything from ambient techno to hardstyle. A theoretical prompt ("Progressive house, 128 BPM, G minor key, four-on-the-floor kick, sidechained pad, plucked lead arpeggio in 16th notes, i–VI–III–VII progression, analog warmth") specifies the subgenre ("progressive house"), the tempo ("128 BPM"), the key ("G minor"), the rhythmic foundation ("four-on-the-floor kick"), the texture ("sidechained pad"), the melodic behavior ("plucked lead arpeggio in 16th notes"), the progression ("i–VI–III–VII"), and the production character ("analog warmth"). A vague jazz prompt ("Jazz") might produce smooth jazz, bebop, or free jazz. A theoretical prompt ("Bebop-influenced jazz trio, 180 BPM, B♭ major key, ii–V–I progressions with secondary dominants, walking upright bass, ride cymbal swing pattern, piano comping with rootless voicings") specifies the style ("bebop-influenced"), the tempo ("180 BPM"), the key ("B♭ major"), the harmonic vocabulary ("ii–V–I progressions with secondary dominants"), the instrumentation ("walking upright bass," "ride cymbal," "piano"), and the performance practice ("swing pattern," "rootless voicings").

The research on LLM music theory performance confirms this. A 2025 study by Pond and Fujinaga tested ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini on Royal Conservatory of Music Level 6 theory questions—intervals, chord identification, key detection, cadence classification, and metrical analysis. Without contextual support, the best model scored only 52%. With carefully designed in-context learning prompts and chain-of-thought worked examples, Claude's performance rose to 75%. The model's theory competence is a function of the prompt's theory competence. A student who can construct the right context and reasoning steps can teach the model in real time; a student who cannot will receive fluent-sounding but unreliable answers.
