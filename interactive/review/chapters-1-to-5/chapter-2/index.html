Chapter 2: Why the Machine Needs Your Theory

2.1 LLMs Are Not Theory-Native

Large language models do not "know" harmony the way a trained musician does. They know statistical associations between words and audio features. When a prompt says "jazz," the model activates a cluster of features—seventh chords, swung rhythms, brass timbres, ii–V–I progressions—that co-occur with the label "jazz" in its training data. But the model does not know why a ii–V–I resolves, or what makes a chord a secondary dominant. It reproduces the surface patterns without the underlying grammar.

This is why explicit theory in the prompt matters. A prompt that says "use a ii–V–I progression in C major" gives the model a specific chord sequence to target. A prompt that says "jazz harmony" gives it a genre label and hopes for the best. The difference is measurable. Studies found that GPT-4 followed instructions for chord progressions about 90% of the time when the specific notes were given, but success dropped to around 20% when the instructions were indirect.

The same pattern holds for other theoretical parameters. A prompt that specifies "A minor key" is more reliably honored than a prompt that says "sad." A prompt that specifies "120 BPM" is more reliably honored than a prompt that says "upbeat." A prompt that specifies "Phrygian mode with ♭2" is more reliably honored than a prompt that says "Spanish." The model's training data contains far more examples of "A minor" than of "sad," and far more examples of "120 BPM" than of "upbeat." The theoretical term is a higher-resolution signal than the affective adjective.

Consider the difference between these two prompts:

Indirect (≈20% success):

Style: "Jazz ballad with sophisticated harmony"

Direct (≈90% success):

Style: "Jazz ballad, B♭ major, ii–V–I progression: [Cm7] [F7] [B♭maj7], soft brushes, upright bass, piano comping"

The first gives the model a genre and a vague quality ("sophisticated harmony"). The second gives it a key ("B♭ major"), a specific progression ("ii–V–I: Cm7–F7–B♭maj7"), and a performance context ("soft brushes, upright bass, piano comping"). The model does not need to infer what "sophisticated harmony" means; it has a concrete target. The theory is the difference between a suggestion and a specification.

A true ii–V–I implies stepwise inner voices, bass moving by descending fifths, and tritones resolving—not just labels. When you hear AI produce D♭7 → G♭maj7 where C♭ should resolve to B♭ but doesn't, you need functional ears to catch it.

2.2 The Pedagogical Problem

If LLMs are not theory-native, then teaching music theory in the AI age cannot be a matter of transmitting facts to be memorized. It must be a matter of training students to externalize their theoretical reasoning—to make key, function, cadence, and voice-leading assumptions explicit, in the correct sequence, using the correct representational format. The model's output becomes a mirror: where it diverges from the specification, the student learns which theoretical distinction was implicit in their thinking but not stated in the prompt.

Consider a simple exercise. Give a student the chord sequence Am–Dm–E7–Am and ask them to prompt a model to produce a correct Roman numeral analysis. A student who writes "analyze this progression" will get a generic answer, perhaps one that mislabels E7 as a diatonic chord or fails to identify the cadence. A student who writes the following will get a rigorous one:

"You are a music theory tutor. Analyze the progression Am–Dm–E7–Am. Step 1: Identify the key by checking the final chord and the presence of the leading tone. Step 2: Label each chord with its Roman numeral and quality. Step 3: Identify the function of E7 (is it diatonic or a secondary dominant?). Step 4: Name the cadence. Show each step explicitly before giving the final analysis."

The second prompt externalizes the analytical process. It tells the model how to reason, not just what to produce. The student who can write this prompt has demonstrated that they know what a Roman numeral analysis involves—key identification, chord labeling, function assignment, cadence classification—and in what order. This is the dialectic model of theory pedagogy: learning theory is learning to prompt, and refining prompts is practicing theory.

The model's output is not the end of the exercise; it is the beginning of the diagnostic. Did the model identify A minor as the key? Did it label E7 as V/iv (a secondary dominant of the subdominant) or as something else? Did it name the final cadence as authentic? Each answer is evidence about what the model has learned and what the student has specified. If the model mislabels E7, the student must ask: was the prompt ambiguous? Did I fail to specify that E7 should be analyzed as a secondary dominant? Or did the model simply not know? The answer determines the next revision.

Traditional pedagogy is criticized for teaching students about harmony rather than teaching them to do or make harmony. AI flips this.

2.3 Prompt Drift and the Need for Anchors

Research comparing Claude, GPT-4, and Gemini for music tasks found that prompt drift—the gradual degradation of constraints across iterative revisions—is a real problem. A prompt that starts with clear constraints ("indie-pop, 118 BPM, E major, I–V–vi–IV") may, after several revisions, become vague ("something like a sad indie song but make it a bit more upbeat, maybe with some synths"). Each revision loosens a constraint, and the model's output drifts toward the average of the new, looser prompt.

The solution is anchoring: restating critical constraints at every level of the prompt, so that even if one layer drifts, the others hold. Consider the difference:

Drift-prone (constraints fade after two revisions):

Style: "Something like a sad indie song but make it a bit more upbeat, maybe with some synths"

Anchored (constraints restated at every level):

Style: "Indie-pop, 118 BPM, E major key, I–V–vi–IV progression, bright synth pads, driving acoustic guitar, no distorted guitars, no trap hi-hats, warm male vocals"

The anchored prompt restates the genre ("indie-pop"), the tempo ("118 BPM"), the key ("E major"), the progression ("I–V–vi–IV"), the instrumentation ("bright synth pads, driving acoustic guitar"), the negative constraints ("no distorted guitars, no trap hi-hats"), and the vocal quality ("warm male vocals"). If the model drifts on one parameter—say, it adds distorted guitars—the other constraints pull it back. If the model drifts on the progression—say, it substitutes a different chord sequence—the key declaration keeps the output diatonic. The redundancy is the anchor.

System may change melody, rhythm, arrangement at the same time. Treat harmonic language as control input, then verify rendered audio.

In harmony pedagogy, this translates to a lesson about redundancy and anchoring: a theoretically well-formed prompt restates critical constraints at multiple points, not because the model is "stupid," but because constraint maintenance is a property of the prompt's theoretical precision. Students who learn harmony without prompt awareness may produce elegant prose descriptions that fail under iteration. Students who learn harmony as a specification language—with explicit fields, declared assumptions, and testable outputs—develop a more durable and transferable theoretical literacy.
