Chapter 20: Modulation — The Architecture of Key Change

20.1 Types of Modulation

Direct (phrase) modulation moves suddenly to a new key. Common-chord (pivot-chord) modulation uses a chord diatonic in both keys. Common-tone modulation reduces the texture to a single common pitch. Chromatic mediant modulation connects chords a third apart. Sequential modulation repeats a phrase at a new pitch level.

20.2 Prompt Implementation by Modulation Type

Direct modulation:

Style: "Pop anthem, 120 BPM, C major, direct modulation up a whole step to D major for the final chorus, key change, full band, stacked vocals"

Pivot-chord modulation:

Style: "Classical, 100 BPM, C major modulating to G major via pivot chord Am (vi in C, ii in G), string quartet"

Common-tone modulation:

Style: "Romantic art song, 72 BPM, common-tone modulation from C major to A♭ major via the note C, piano and voice"

Chromatic mediant modulation:

Style: "Film score, 80 BPM, chromatic mediant shift from C major to A♭ major, sudden lift, strings and brass"

Sequential modulation:

Style: "Jazz, 140 BPM, sequential modulation: ii–V–I in C, then up a whole step in D, then up another step in E, piano trio"

20.3 Near-Key and Distant-Key Modulation

Distant-key prompt example:

Style: "Cinematic, 90 BPM, C major modulating to F♯ major (tritone relationship) at the bridge, dramatic, unsettling, full orchestra"

20.4 The Modulation Diagnostic

Test: Specify a modulation in the prompt. Generate, then analyze whether the new tonic is actually established or whether the model merely chromatically colors the old key. A successful modulation will show a new leading tone, a cadence in the new key, and a pivot or common tone.
