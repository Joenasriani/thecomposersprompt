32.1 The Theoretical Brief

Compose a 32-bar passage that moves from Phrygian darkness to Dorian hope, using appoggiatura-based melody, a chromatic mediant modulation, and parallel planing in the bridge.

Derived prompt specification:

  • Opening (Level 1): E Phrygian, 72 BPM, dark, solo voice and drone. Interval character: minor 2nds and tritones for roughness.
  • Melody (Level 2): Accented appoggiaturas resolving by step, stepwise passing tones.
  • Modulation (Level 4): Chromatic mediant shift from E Phrygian to C Dorian at bar 17.
  • Bridge (Level 3): Parallel planing of maj7 chords, non-functional color.
  • Closing (Level 1 & 4): C Dorian, hopeful, full ensemble, orchestral build.

Suno prompt:

Style: "Dark cinematic folk, 72 BPM, E Phrygian mode with ♭2, accented appoggiaturas in the melody, solo voice and drone, chromatic mediant modulation to C Dorian at the bridge, parallel maj7 planing in the bridge, building to hopeful full ensemble"

Udio prompt:

"dark cinematic folk, solo voice and drone, accented appoggiaturas, minor 2nd and tritone dissonance, key of E Phrygian, chromatic mediant modulation to C Dorian at the bridge, parallel maj7 planing, 72 BPM, building to hopeful full ensemble"

Stable Audio prompt (instrumental version):

"dark cinematic folk, solo cello and drone, accented appoggiaturas, minor 2nd and tritone dissonance, Phrygian on E, chromatic mediant modulation to C Dorian at the bridge, parallel maj7 planing, 72 BPM, TrackType: Instrument, building to hopeful full ensemble, noise level 0.5"

32.2 The Diagnostic Analysis

Generate the passage and answer the following:

  1. Interval character: Does the opening use dissonant intervals (minor 2nds, tritones) congruent with Phrygian darkness?
  2. Mode affect: Does the shift to Dorian produce the empirical "positive emotion" profile, or does the model flatten the modal difference?
  3. Modulation: Is the chromatic mediant shift audible as a new key, or does it sound like a chromatic coloration of E Phrygian?
  4. NCTs: Are the appoggiaturas accented and resolved by step, or does the model smooth them into passing tones?
  5. Parallelism: Does the bridge exhibit parallel chord motion, or does it revert to functional voice leading?

The written reflection is the assessment: which perceptual distinctions survived the prompt-to-audio pipeline, and what does each survival or failure reveal about the model's internalization of the theory?

32.3 The Hijaz Exercise (Non-Western Capstone)

The Western capstone above can be complemented by a non-Western exercise that tests the same specification discipline on a system the model's training data represents less thoroughly. The Hijaz maqam exercise is the recommended variant.

The brief: Create a 3-bar modal MIDI in a DAW on D Hijaz. Upload the MIDI as reference audio to Stable Audio audio-to-audio with noise level 0.5. Prompt with the interval sequence.

The Stable Audio prompt:

"Solo oud, Hijaz maqam on D: D–E♯–F–G–A–B♭–C–D, emphasize E♯–F and A–B♭ neighbor motion, 10/8, no Western harmony—only heterophonic variation, TrackType: Instrument, 66 BPM, noise level 0.5"

Evaluation: Compare the generated audio to the MIDI reference using a spectrum analyzer. Does the output preserve the augmented second between E♯ and F? Does it avoid adding functional harmony (V–I cadences, tertian chords)? Does the heterophonic texture survive, or does the model substitute a homophonic accompaniment?

The lesson is the same as the Western capstone: which perceptual distinctions survive the pipeline, and what does each survival or failure reveal about the model's internalization of the theory? The difference is that the Hijaz exercise tests the model's ability to honor a non-Western specification, which is a harder test of the specification discipline because the training data is thinner.

Conclusion: The Invariant and the Variable

The crescendo from Level 1 to Level 5 is not a sequence of topics to be "covered." It is a deepening of the same act: making a theoretical distinction explicit enough that a model can attempt it, and then reading the model's attempt as evidence about the distinction itself.

At Level 1, the student learns that the key declaration is a constraint and that the character of an interval is a feeling the prompt can name. At Level 5, the student learns that the entire workflow—specification, generation, diagnosis, and revision—is the theory exercise, and that the professional musician's advantage in the age of AI is not superior generation, but superior specification and diagnosis.

The prompt examples in this book are not "magic templates" to be copied without thought. They are theory exercises in executable form. Each bracket, each Roman numeral, each voicing annotation, each modal descriptor is a theoretical decision made explicit. When the model's output diverges from the specification, the divergence is not a failure of the prompt—it is data about which musical distinctions the model has learned and which it has not. The student who can read that data has learned both the theory and the machine.

The research is clear that LLMs can be taught music theory through prompts, but the deeper finding is that the act of teaching the machine is itself a theory pedagogy. The student who can diagnose why a model mislabels a cadence, and revise the prompt to fix it, has demonstrated a more robust understanding of cadence than the student who can only label it correctly on an exam. The student who can hear that the model has flattened a Phrygian mode into Aeolian, and revise the prompt to restore the ♭2, has demonstrated a more robust understanding of modal color than the student who can only list the modes in order.

The composer's prompt is not a replacement for the score. It is the score's next evolution—a specification language for a new kind of performer, one that reads probability distributions instead of notation, and one that requires the composer to know the theory more explicitly, more precisely, and more diagnostically than ever before. The invariant is the theory. The variable is the syntax. The musician who masters both has the instrument.

What theory gives you that AI can't. "AI doesn't 'follow' functional harmony—it observes how humans have followed it, then generalizes." "The biggest reason to learn basic music theory isn't to write better Suno prompts — it's to understand what Suno gives you back."

  • Evaluation, not generation. Theory improves comparison and revision decisions. "Theory becomes useful when you want to understand why one result works better than another, diagnose a problem or make a more controlled revision."
  • Intentionality over randomness. AI optimizes for familiarity, not grammar. Skilled users interrogate, refine, reclaim harmonic agency.
  • Future-proofing. Berklee's new 4-week AI for Music and Audio course is built on "hands-on with AI tools for music analysis, production, composition" with no coding required, but prerequisites still include "basic working knowledge of songwriting (what a chord progression is)." AI literacy assumes theory literacy.

AI for music education in 2026 is described as "interactive, responsive, inspiring"—AI breaks down harmonic structure, explains relationships, shows tension/resolution, and generates teaching material optimized for learning goals by identifying patterns in thousands of songs. But it works only if the learner can ask the right question.

The mantra. Teach students this, adapted from the research:

Harmony is not a list of emotion presets. It is movement: what feels settled, what leaves home, what creates expectation, and what finally resolves.

If they can hear home, departure, and expectation — they can prompt anything. If they can't, no prompt engineering will save them.
