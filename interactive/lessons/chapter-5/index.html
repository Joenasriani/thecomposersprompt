Chapter 5: Scales and Modes — The Emotional Palettes

5.1 Empirical Findings on Modal Affect

A controlled study of church modal music found significant, systematic differences in emotional response: Dorian significantly enhanced positive emotions; Lydian and Mixolydian effectively alleviated negative emotions; Phrygian, Aeolian, and Locrian significantly increased negative emotional responses, with Locrian evoking the strongest negative emotions. A separate pilot study (N=142) found distinct semantic fields for each mode: Ionian was linked to "life" and "joy"; Phrygian to "solitude," "sadness," and "fear"; Lydian to "nature" and "joy"; and Aeolian to "mystery" and "sadness".

Historical theorists were less consistent. Lippius (1612) considered Ionian, Lydian, and Mixolydian "happy," and Dorian, Phrygian, and Aeolian "weak, sad, and serious." Gumpelzhaimer (1591) called Dorian "cheerful," Phrygian "severe," Lydian "harsh," and Mixolydian "impatient." The lesson for the composer is that modal affect is real but context-dependent: the mode sets a palette, and the prompt must reinforce it with congruent emotional adjectives, tempo, and texture.

5.2 A Mode-by-Mode Prompt Lexicon

Mode Characteristic Pitch Emotional Field Prompt-Ready Description
Ionian (major) Natural 4, natural 7 Joy, life, brightness "bright major key, joyful, resolved"
Dorian Raised 6 Positive, hopeful minor; jazzy "Dorian mode, minor with a raised 6th, hopeful, jazzy"
Phrygian ♭2 Solitude, sadness, fear; Spanish/metal "Phrygian mode, ♭2, Spanish, dark, exotic"
Lydian ♯4 Nature, joy, dreamlike "Lydian mode, ♯4, dreamy, ethereal, floating"
Mixolydian ♭7 Happy, relaxed, bluesy "Mixolydian mode, ♭7, relaxed, bluesy, open"
Aeolian (natural minor) ♭3, ♭6, ♭7 Mystery, sadness "Aeolian mode, natural minor, mysterious, sad"
Locrian ♭2, ♭5 Maximum instability, negative "Locrian mode, ♭5, unstable, dark, unsettling"

The "characteristic pitch" is the note that distinguishes the mode from its nearest neighbor. If you want the model to produce a specific mode, you must specify the characteristic pitch—otherwise the model may default to the nearest more common mode (Aeolian for Dorian, Ionian for Lydian, etc.).

5.3 Mode-Specific Prompt Examples

Dorian (positive minor):

Style: "Jazz-funk, D Dorian mode, minor with a raised 6th, hopeful and groovy, clavinet, 110 BPM"

Phrygian (Spanish/metal):

Style: "Flamenco-metal, E Phrygian mode, ♭2 for Spanish tension, nylon-string and distorted guitar, 95 BPM"

Lydian (dreamlike):

Style: "Film score, F Lydian mode, ♯4 for ethereal floating, strings and harp, 70 BPM"

Mixolydian (bluesy/open):

Style: "Southern rock, G Mixolydian mode, ♭7 for relaxed blues feel, slide guitar, 100 BPM"

Locrian (unstable):

Style: "Experimental metal, B Locrian mode, ♭5 instability, dissonant chords, 160 BPM"

5.4 Modal Drone and Pedal

A drone or pedal tone sustains a single pitch while harmonies change above it. This is especially effective for establishing a modal center without functional harmony: the ear hears the drone as the tonic, and the changing upper voices are heard as color rather than progression.

Drone prompt example:

Style: "Ambient modal, sustained D drone in the bass, Dorian colors above, 60 BPM, no functional cadences, evolving textures"

Pedal-point prompt example:

Style: "Post-rock, pedal point on the dominant (A), changing chords above, 80 BPM, building tension"

5.5 The Mode-Feeling Diagnostic

Test: Write the same melody in three prompts: (A) Ionian with "joyful" adjectives; (B) Dorian with "hopeful minor" adjectives; (C) Phrygian with "dark, Spanish" adjectives. Generate all three and analyze the semantic field of each output. The lesson: mode is a palette, and the prompt must name both the palette and the feeling it is meant to evoke.
