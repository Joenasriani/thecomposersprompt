7.1 What the Model Has Internalized

Suno is trained on popular music and "naturally gravitates toward progressions that are common in popular music." These five work reliably with bracketed chords:

Progression Roman Numerals Example in C Major Prompt-Ready Bracket String
Pop I–V–vi–IV C–G–Am–F [C] … [G] … [Am] … [F]
Sensitive vi–IV–I–V Am–F–C–G [Am] … [F] … [C] … [G]
Axis I–vi–IV–V C–Am–F–G [C] … [Am] … [F] … [G]
Folk I–IV–V–I C–F–G–C [C] … [F] … [G] … [C]
50s I–vi–IV–V C–Am–F–G (same as axis, different rhythm)

The theoretical lesson is not "these progressions are the only good ones" but "these are the progressions whose functional logic the model has internalized from its training corpus."

7.4 The Translation Matrix

This is the core teaching device of the book. Students don't need to memorize; they need to translate. The matrix maps what you hear or want onto the theory term, the way the AI responds best, and a weak-vs-strong prompt comparison.

Critical limitation to teach: "AI tools like Suno generally do not respond well to specific music theory notation. Instead of prompting I-V-vi-IV, prompt the feeling and genre associated with it." And: "System may change melody, rhythm, arrangement at the same time. Treat harmonic language as control input, then verify rendered audio."

Forcing non-major: Never rely solely on "Dorian" or "maqam Hijaz." Pair with concrete anchors: root + interval sequence, reference piece ("like Miles Davis's 'So What'"), or MIDI clip.

7.5 Interval-Array Notation

For precise modal specification, the interval array is a machine-readable anchor:

  • Major (Ionian): [0,2,4,5,7,9,11]
  • Dorian: [0,2,3,5,7,9,10]
  • Phrygian: [0,1,3,5,7,8,10]
  • Lydian: [0,2,4,6,7,9,11]
  • Mixolydian: [0,2,4,5,7,9,10]
  • Aeolian: [0,2,3,5,7,8,10]
  • Locrian: [0,1,3,5,6,8,10]

The array is specified in the prompt as a sequence of semitone offsets from the root. It is more reliable than the bare mode name because it gives the model an explicit pitch collection, not just a label.

Prompt example:

Style: "Modal jazz, D Dorian, intervals [0,2,3,5,7,9,10], emphasize minor 7th, Rhodes and muted guitar, 90 BPM, no major cadences"

7.6 The Hijaz Case Study (Non-Western)

Western modes are the default, but the same specification techniques work for non-Western maqam and raga systems. The Hijaz maqam is a useful case study because its interval structure (augmented second between the second and third degrees) is distinctive and the model's training data includes enough flamenco, Arabic, and Turkish music to make the specification operational.

Full specification:

Style: "Hijaz maqam on D: D–E♯–F–G–A–B♭–C–D; emphasize E♯–F and A–B♭ neighbor motion; texture: solo oud; tempo 66 BPM, 10/8; no Western harmony—only heterophonic variation"

The key moves:

  1. Name the maqam and root ("Hijaz maqam on D")
  2. Specify the full pitch collection ("D–E♯–F–G–A–B♭–C–D")
  3. Emphasize the characteristic neighbor motions ("E♯–F and A–B♭")
  4. Specify the texture ("solo oud")
  5. Specify the meter ("10/8")
  6. Negative constraint ("no Western harmony—only heterophonic variation")

The negative constraint is critical: without it, the model will likely add functional harmony that is alien to the maqam tradition. The prompt specifies not just what to include but what to exclude.
